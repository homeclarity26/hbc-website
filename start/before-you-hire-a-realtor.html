Before you hire a realtor: renovate or sell?

May 2026 · By Adam Kilgore, Hometown Builders Club

Most homeowners in Hudson, Bath, Fairlawn, and Cuyahoga Falls call a realtor first when they start thinking about a move. It's the natural call. A friend in real estate, a name from a neighbor, the agent who sent the holiday calendar. Within a week you're looking at comps and talking about a listing date.

Here's the part that usually doesn't get said. The realtor's job, the work they're trained for and paid for, is selling homes. Their first meeting with you is built around that. They are not built to walk you through the math of staying. That's not a bad thing. It's just the shape of the business.

So before you hire a realtor, run the question they aren't going to run for you: does it actually make more sense to renovate the home you're in than to sell it and buy something else? For a lot of Summit County homeowners, the answer surprises them.

When a realtor's first call is the right one

Some situations make calling a realtor first the obviously correct move:

  • You're moving for a job, a family member, or a school decision that has already settled. The home is leaving your hands either way.
  • You've genuinely outgrown the house. More kids, an aging parent moving in, a lifestyle change that adding square footage won't fix.
  • The home is already in clean, sale-ready shape. Recent updates, working systems, no major deferred maintenance.
  • You want out of Summit County entirely. Climate, taxes, family location, retirement.

In any of those cases, talk to a realtor first. Get the listing process moving and don't overthink it.

When a realtor's first call is the wrong one

The trickier situation, and the one I see most often, is the homeowner who's "thinking about selling" because the kitchen feels dated or the bathrooms are tired or the first floor is chopped up and dark. They feel the friction with the home every day. Selling presents itself as the cure.

It often isn't. Three reasons.

One, the cost of moving. In Summit County, the all-in cost to sell a $500,000 home and buy a comparable $500,000 home is typically $50,000 to $80,000 once you add up agent commissions, closing costs, moving expenses, the inspection-driven repairs the buyer will demand, and the small stuff that piles up. You spend that money to end up with a different version of the same square footage.

Two, the inventory problem. Hudson, Bath, Fairlawn, and Montrose-Ghent are tight markets in 2026. The listings that come on are often the ones with deeper issues than yours. The "better" home you're imagining buying may not exist on the timeline you have, and the bidding when it does appear is real.

Three, the renovation alternative is almost always cheaper than the perception. A homeowner who feels like they need a "new house" usually means they need a new kitchen, a redone primary bath, and an opened-up first floor. In Summit County, that package runs $80,000 to $200,000 depending on finish level. Compare that against the $50,000 to $80,000 friction cost of moving, plus a likely buy-up price on the next home, and the math often comes out in favor of staying.

The honest math, not a sales pitch. I'm a remodeler. I have a clear bias toward you staying and renovating. I'm also a homeowner in Cuyahoga Falls. The number of people I've talked out of a remodel because selling was cleaner is real. The Home Clarity Report runs both numbers and tells you which one fits your situation.

What it actually costs to make a Summit County home sale-ready

Most homes in our market need a smaller pre-sale package than homeowners assume. The buyer is not paying you back dollar-for-dollar on a renovated kitchen. They are paying you back on the absence of red flags during the inspection.

What rarely pays back at sale

  • Full kitchen remodel done specifically to sell. Returns typically 50 to 70 percent of cost. The buyer either accepts your finishes or doesn't.
  • Full primary bath remodel done specifically to sell. Same dynamic. Strong only if the existing bath is genuinely failing.
  • Custom built-ins, finished basement upgrades, screened-in porch additions. Personal-use projects. Buyers value them, but they pay for the home, not the addition.

The exception: if you're going to live in the home for three or more additional years, those projects are not "to sell." They're for you. The math works differently.

What to do before you sign a listing agreement

  1. Decide whether you actually want to sell or whether you want a different version of your current home. If the second one is honestly true, talk to a remodeler first.
  2. Get an independent assessment of the home's systems and what they really need. Roof, HVAC, plumbing, electrical, foundation, windows. The realtor's CMA doesn't cover this. The buyer's inspector will.
  3. Get realistic cost ranges for the renovation alternative. Not a contractor sales meeting. A written number you can compare against the move number.
  4. Then call your realtor. If selling is still the right answer, you'll have the data to do it cleanly. If staying is the right answer, you saved yourself a listing and a move you didn't need.

Frequently asked questions

Should I renovate before selling or sell as-is?

It depends on what's outdated, what condition the systems are in, and what your local market rewards. In Summit County, fresh paint, refinished floors, deep cleaning, and curb appeal almost always pay for themselves at sale. Major projects like a full kitchen or primary bath remodel rarely return more than 50 to 70 percent of cost when done specifically to sell. The Home Clarity Report runs both numbers for your specific home before you commit either way.

Why shouldn't I just call a realtor first?

You can. Just understand what you'll get. A realtor's job is to list and sell homes. Their first conversation with you is shaped by that business. They are unlikely to walk you through the renovate-versus-sell decision in a way that fairly weighs staying. That's not dishonest, it's the shape of the work. If you've already decided to sell, a realtor first is the right call. If you haven't, talk to a remodeler first.

How much do typical pre-sale repairs cost in Summit County?

For most Summit County homes built in the 1960s to 1990s, a sensible pre-sale package runs $8,000 to $25,000. That covers fresh interior paint, deep cleaning, landscaping, minor plumbing and electrical fixes, and one or two targeted cosmetic upgrades. Going beyond that into full renovations rarely makes financial sense unless you plan to live in the home for at least three more years.

What does the Home Clarity Report tell me a realtor won't?

The Report documents your home's actual systems, the realistic cost to fix or upgrade them, and which projects deliver value at resale versus which are personal-use upgrades. It also includes a 3D scan, written scopes for any work you'd consider, and current Summit County pricing. A realtor's CMA tells you what comparable homes sold for. The Report tells you what your home is, what it needs, and what your options are.

When does it make sense to hire a realtor first?

When you've already decided to sell, when you're moving for a job or family reason that takes selling off the table as a question, or when your home is already in clean, sale-ready shape. In those cases, calling a realtor first is the right move.
